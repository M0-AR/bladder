The abstract

A small camera. A hard problem. A pipeline you can audit.

Bladder cancer is often found through cystoscopy, a procedure in which a thin camera inspects the lining of the bladder. Most new diagnoses are non-muscle-invasive, and recurrence is common enough that surveillance becomes a long relationship. The procedure is operator-dependent, the video is rich, and the documentation is often thinner than the evidence deserves.

This repository turns that workflow into reproducible software. It extracts annotated frames, converts annotations, splits data with a written manifest, trains a frame classifier, runs a single-class lesion detector, and reports metrics a clinical reader can inspect: sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and a confusion matrix. It also includes an interactive 3D environment that teaches the logic behind those numbers.

Every stage is a file contract. Every result is traceable to a seed, a split, a threshold, and a commit. Nothing here is a medical device, and the project says so before you have to ask.

“The most important sentence in medical AI is not ‘the model is accurate.’ It is ‘accurate on whom, compared with what, at which threshold, and with what uncertainty?’”

🌱 Beginner guide

Read this and you are a professional.

No medical degree. No machine-learning PhD. Seven ideas, in the order they actually matter. By the end, you will know more than most interview candidates.

01 · THE CAMERA

Cystoscopy is the front door

A thin scope enters through the urethra and looks at the bladder wall. The result is a video, not a single photo. Lighting, angle, and the operator's hand all change what you see.

Why it matters: the model sees frames, but the clinician sees a procedure.
02 · THE MAP

Annotations turn pixels into labels

A clinician draws a box around a suspicious region and attaches a label. That box is a claim about the world. If the box is wrong, the model learns the wrong lesson efficiently.

Why it matters: annotation quality is a ceiling on model quality.
03 · THE TEXTBOOK

A dataset is a curated textbook

Images plus labels are the examples. A dataset is not neutral: it reflects who was imaged, on which device, at which hospital, and who decided what counted as disease.

Why it matters: “works on my dataset” is a statement about the dataset first.
04 · THE EXAM

Split by patient, not by frame

Frames from one procedure look alike. If some go into training and others into test, the model can memorise the patient and look brilliant. That is leakage, not learning.

Why it matters: a patient-level split is the difference between a demo and evidence.
05 · THE STUDENT

The model finds patterns, not meaning

A convolutional network learns textures, edges, colours, and shapes. It does not know what cancer is. It knows which pixel patterns were labelled cancer in the data it saw.

Why it matters: domain shift is not a bug; it is the default.
06 · THE REPORT CARD

One accuracy number is a red flag

Sensitivity asks how many true cases you found. Specificity asks how many healthy cases you cleared. PPV and NPV depend on how common the disease is in the room. Always ask for the confusion matrix.

Why it matters: the threshold is a choice, and the choice has consequences.
07 · THE CLINIC

A model is not a product

Clinical use needs a defined intended use, external validation, human-factors evidence, quality systems, cybersecurity, and a regulatory pathway. A notebook is the beginning of that work, not the end.

Why it matters: “the AUC is high” has never treated a patient.
THE ONE THING

Ask “compared with what?”

Compared with a second cystoscopy? With histopathology? With a generalist? With the same model at a different threshold? The comparison is the claim.

Why it matters: precision without a reference is decoration.

Questions

The honest FAQ.

Is this a medical device?

No. It is research and education software. It has not been cleared, certified, or approved by any regulator, and it must not be used for diagnosis, screening, or treatment. Read DISCLAIMER.md.

Does it include a trained classifier?

No. The repository ships a single-class TFLite detector and the training code for a three-class frame classifier. To use the classifier, you train it on data you are allowed to use. This is stated in MODEL_FACTS.md.

Why are the classes NMIBC, Normal, and Ta?

That is the original research convention. It is not a mutually exclusive clinical taxonomy: Ta is a stage within NMIBC. A clinically meaningful system would separate detection, staging, grading, and histology, and would be validated against pathology. The glossary explains the terms.

What does “patient-level split” mean and why does it matter?

All frames from one patient go into exactly one split. If frames from the same procedure appear in both training and test, the model can memorise the patient and look better than it is. That is data leakage. See EVALUATION.md.

Can I use it commercially?

The code is MIT licensed. You may use, modify, and distribute it commercially, provided you keep the copyright and permission notice. The licence does not grant rights to patient data, third-party datasets, trademarks, or regulatory approval. You remain responsible for your own evidence and compliance.

Does the website collect data or use patient images?

No. The site is static, has no analytics, and uses synthetic or stylised visuals. The simulator is a teaching model with a synthetic cohort, not the shipped detector. Narration uses the browser's local speech synthesis.

How do I cite it?

Use the metadata in CITATION.cff. If you use a public dataset, cite that dataset too. If you build on the detector, state that it is single-class and unvalidated.

How can I contribute?

Read CONTRIBUTING.md. The rules that matter most: no patient data, no invented clinical claims, and a reproducible command for every result. Security issues go through the private advisory flow in SECURITY.md.
